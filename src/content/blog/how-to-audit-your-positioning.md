Traffic but no conversions? Demos with smart people who don't buy? Outreach that gets replies but not meetings?

These are clear symptoms. And the diagnosis is bad positioning. Not always: sometimes it's sales execution, sometimes it's expectation-setting earlier in the funnel. But when the same pattern shows up across multiple parts of the pipeline, positioning is usually what's underneath.

Positioning is the set of decisions that determine how your product appears in the mind of the right buyer. When it's right, the right people show up, understand what you do immediately, and feel compelled to take a step. When it's off, you get activity without traction. Here's how to tell the difference, and what to fix.

## The three signs your positioning needs work

**Sign 1: You get a lot of "that's interesting" on calls.**

"Interesting" is a polite way of saying "I don't know what to do with this." It means the person you're talking to can see what you do, but can't connect it to a problem they're actively trying to solve. Good positioning skips interesting entirely. The right buyer hears your pitch and says: "that's exactly what I've been looking for."

One caveat: "interesting" isn't always a positioning issue. Sometimes the positioning is fine but the demo is framed as a tour of features instead of a solution to a problem the buyer already named. If you're confident the message is sharp and you still get "interesting," look at how the calls are being run before you rewrite the positioning.

**Sign 2: Your customers are telling your story for you, and no two versions agree.**

Ask three of your best customers to explain what you do. If you get three different answers, the issue usually isn't that the positioning is wrong. It's that you haven't owned the narrative clearly enough, so your customers are filling in the gap themselves.

That's what happens by default. Customers build their own story out of what they use, what they tell their team, and what they remember from the sales call. If your homepage, pitch, and content don't give them a story strong enough to repeat, they'll keep inventing one. The result is that every customer becomes a slightly different translation of your product, and the translations don't compound into a recognizable market position.

The fix isn't to force a single story on your customers. It's to own the narrative so clearly that theirs starts to align.

**Sign 3: Your best customers came through referrals, but you can't replicate it.**

Word-of-mouth works because someone described your product so well that it clicked for the next person. If that description lives only in the head of one satisfied customer, and not in your homepage, pitch, or content, you haven't yet externalized it. The positioning exists. You just haven't found it yet.

## The four questions to answer

[April Dunford's positioning framework](https://www.aprildunford.com/obviously-awesome) is the clearest treatment of this I've found. It centers on four questions:

**Who are the customers who buy fastest and stay longest?** Start there. Not the aspirational customer. The actual best one you have right now.

**What are the real alternatives?** What would your customer do if your product didn't exist? "Do nothing" is an alternative. A spreadsheet is an alternative. The alternative defines the competitive context, and that context shapes how your product should be positioned.

**What do you do that no realistic alternative does?** Not all your features. The one thing that only you offer to the people who need it most.

**What's the measurable outcome of that thing?** Not "saves time" or "reduces complexity." Something the buyer can defend in a board meeting: a percentage, a number, a concrete before-and-after.

Run these questions against your existing customers. Record the conversations. The answers won't come from your head, they'll come from theirs.

## The quick test

Write one sentence that completes this structure: "For [specific who] who [specific situation], [product name] is the [category] that [differentiator], unlike [alternative]."

Now send it to your three best customers and ask: does this describe why you chose us?

If they say yes, you have a positioning statement. If they say "mostly, but..." that "but" is the work. It's telling you exactly what to refine.

If the sentence sounds like it could apply to three of your competitors, it needs another round. Positioning is only working when the right person reads it and thinks: that's for me, and nothing else does this quite this way.
