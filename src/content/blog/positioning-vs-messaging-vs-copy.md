Every founder I work with has rewritten their homepage at least twice in the last year. The third rewrite is usually the one that gets me a Slack message: "It still doesn't convert. Can you take a look?"

I take a look. The copy is fine. The copy was fine the first two times too. That's the problem.

Positioning, messaging, and copy are three different things sitting on top of each other. When you edit the wrong layer, nothing moves. And most founders spend six months editing the top layer while the bottom one is what's actually broken.

This is the guide I wish I could hand every seed-stage founder before they touch the homepage again.

## The three-layer problem

Here's the pattern. Founder ships v1 of the website. Six weeks in, sales calls keep stalling on "so what does it do exactly?" So the founder rewrites the homepage. Six weeks later, same objection. Rewrites again. New tagline, new hero, maybe a new video. Same objection.

By month six, the founder is convinced they need a copywriter. What they actually need is to figure out which layer is broken. A copywriter can genuinely help with that, but only a specific kind: one who sits in on customer calls, reads the reviews, checks what competitors say, and treats positioning as an input to investigate, not a given. Hire someone who "just knows how to write" and you get clever copy. Hire someone who does the research first and you get clear copy. Those are different services, and most founders buy the first one when they need the second.

Because it's almost never copy.

**Positioning** is the strategic decision: what market you're in, who you're for, and how you win.

**Messaging** is the translation layer: how you argue your case, in what order, with what proof.

**Copy** is the words in the room: this headline, this button, this LinkedIn post.

Copy edits can't fix a positioning problem. A positioning doc doesn't substitute for messaging discipline. And a messaging framework, however tight, still needs someone to write the actual sentence that goes on the homepage.

Collapse the three into one and you spend your Tuesdays rewriting the hero section instead of asking whether the category you picked is the one your buyers use.

## Positioning: the category and the win condition

[April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/obviously-awesome) is the person to read here. Her definition, from Obviously Awesome:

> "Positioning is the act of deliberately defining how you are the best at something that a defined market cares a lot about."

Note what she doesn't say. She doesn't say positioning is a tagline. She doesn't say it's a sentence on your website. It's a decision about five things:

1. **Competitive alternatives**: what your buyer would do if you didn't exist. Often a spreadsheet, an intern, or nothing.
2. **Unique attributes**: the specific capabilities you have that the alternatives lack.
3. **Value**: the outcome those attributes enable, with proof.
4. **Target market characteristics**: the buyers who care most about that value.
5. **Market category**: the frame you put yourself in so the buyer's brain knows what to compare you against.

That last one bites hardest. Dunford again:

> "Market categories are one way that customers organize products in their minds. Declaring that your product exists in a certain market category will set off a powerful set of assumptions in customers' minds about who your competitors are, what the functionality of the product should be and what the pricing is like."

ZYGO is a case in point, though I'll be honest about how the call actually got made. Their positioning was already solid before I showed up. This wasn't a rescue job. What was still open was the exact category label: robotics company, software company, or platform. I spent multiple weeks with the founder debating it, and the answer we landed on was "robotics software platform." Ugly on the page. Correct in the buyer's head. But I won't dress up the process as more rigorous than it was. Part of that call went with what was trending in the market at the time, not a scientific read of customer signal. Still, until the label was picked, no copy on the site could work, because copy is downstream of category. You can't write a headline about being "the best X" until X is decided.

Dunford drives the point home:

> "It's important to really understand what customers compare your solution with, because that's the yardstick they use to define 'better.' For example, your solution might be much easier to use than the product that other startups are selling, but if the real alternative in the mind of a customer is Excel, you can't say your product is easier to use unless it is easier to use than a spreadsheet."

Positioning comes first. Everything else waits for it.

## Messaging: the translation layer

Positioning tells you what you're claiming. Messaging is how you build the argument.

A good messaging doc contains: your value propositions (usually three), the proof points behind each one, the objections you expect and how you handle them, a boilerplate paragraph for anyone writing about you, and the primary and secondary keywords your audience actually uses.

The best public example of messaging-as-architecture is [Andy Raskin's strategic narrative](https://andyraskin.com/), the five-part story a company tells: name a big undeniable change, show the winners and losers, tease a promised land, present your capabilities as the way to get there, and prove it with customer evidence. That's not positioning. That's the argument shape you use once positioning is locked. It works for a sales deck, a keynote, a homepage, a fundraising pitch.

Messaging is where founders get stuck the second time around. They have a positioning doc. They know their category. But every time they write about the product, the value props shuffle, the proof points wander, the objection handling contradicts the pitch deck. Sales says one thing, marketing says another, the CEO says a third on stage.

That's a messaging problem. No amount of headline testing will fix it.

## Copy: the words in the room

Copy is channel-specific and disposable. A LinkedIn post, a cold email subject line, an ad, a landing page headline, a webinar title. Different formats, different audiences, different constraints. But all downstream of the same messaging, which is downstream of the same positioning.

When Sophie at ONAH and I sat down for four hours to cascade her ICP into positioning, then positioning into messaging, then messaging into copy, the last hour produced lines like "the inbox no longer sets the agenda" and "stop answering emails, start doing HR work." Those lines land because the three layers below them agreed. If we'd started at the copy layer, we'd have spent four hours arguing about adjectives.

Copy is where the craft lives. But craft applied to the wrong foundation is decoration on a house with no beams.

## How to diagnose which layer is broken

When your homepage isn't working, ask these questions in this order. Don't skip.

**1. Do your customers describe you the same way you describe yourself?**
If no: positioning problem. You've picked a category or a value they don't recognize.

**2. Do your sales calls stall on "so what does it actually do?"**
Positioning problem. The category frame is wrong or missing.

**3. Can two people in your company give the same three-sentence pitch without coordinating?**
If no: messaging problem. The argument architecture isn't shared.

**4. Do your value props change depending on who's presenting?**
Messaging problem. You have opinions, not a framework.

**5. Do the words on the page feel flat, generic, or off-tone, but your team agrees on what you do and why?**
Copy problem. Get a writer.

If you answered "yes" to 1 or 2, don't touch the copy. Go back to positioning. If 3 or 4 landed, don't touch the copy either. Go build the messaging doc. Only if the argument is solid and shared do you get to argue about the headline.

## What happens when you collapse the stack

The [MarketingSherpa B2B case study](https://sherpablog.marketingsherpa.com/) is a textbook example of wrong-layer debugging. Team A/B tested a "mobile-centric" homepage variant. The new version lost. Their post-mortem blamed the headline length: three lines versus two. The layer they never questioned was whether "mobile-centric" was the right positioning frame for that buyer in the first place. They rewrote copy to fix positioning. The test data was noise.

I see this every week. With Sofico, we ended up scheduling recurring one-hour positioning calls, and they were genuinely hard sessions. Not because the team disagreed or lost focus, but because Sofico's real competitive alternative isn't a rival piece of software. It's the outdated system already running in the customer's building, the bespoke solution someone built in-house years ago, and the plain fear of touching either. That's a harder positioning problem than "who else sells what we sell," and it took slow, deliberate work to get right. Positioning is cognitively expensive by nature. It's decision-making under real uncertainty. The reason founders reach for copy edits instead is that copy edits feel productive. You can ship a new headline in twenty minutes. You can't ship a new category in twenty minutes.

Willow is the other side of the same coin. Ongoing "Create post" naming drift, a comparison hub that keeps getting reshuffled, copy edits piling up in Notion. Every individual edit is defensible. The cumulative effect is a product surface that shifts every two weeks because the layer below never got named.

The counter-anchor is [Drift](https://www.drift.com/). Between 2016 and 2019 they repositioned from "live chat" (a crowded, commoditised category) to "Conversational Marketing" (a new category they authored and evangelised through a book and a certification program). ARR went from around $6M in 2017 to $100M+ by 2021, with over $5B in customer pipeline generated in 2021, ending with Vista Equity taking a majority position at a $1B+ valuation. The category changed first. The copy followed. Not the other way around.

## The order matters (flow only runs down)

Positioning gets revisited when the market moves, the product ships something new, or your ICP sharpens. That happens once or twice a year for most seed-stage companies. Not every quarter.

Messaging gets a proper review every quarter. New objections have surfaced. Some proof points aged out. A value prop turned out to be weaker than you thought.

Copy gets rewritten every week. That's fine. That's what copy is for.

Try to run the flow in reverse and you get a beautiful homepage nobody clicks. You end up reverse-engineering positioning from a tagline someone brainstormed on a Friday afternoon. The tagline came from a vibe. The vibe came from what the founder read on Twitter that morning. Now the entire company is aligned to a Twitter vibe.

Dunford, one more time:

> "If we fail at positioning, we fail at marketing and sales. If we fail at marketing and sales, the entire business fails."

Bit dramatic. Also true.

## Where to start if all three feel broken

Start with positioning. Always.

If you're pre-traction or early-traction, you probably don't have a real positioning doc. You have a deck. You have a homepage. You have things you've said on sales calls that seemed to work. That's raw material, not positioning.

The next step is not a copywriter. It's a two-hour session where you name your five components: alternatives, unique attributes, value, target market, category. Get them on paper. Show them to three customers. Ask if it sounds like the thing they bought.

Then, and only then, write the messaging. Then, and only then, write the copy.
